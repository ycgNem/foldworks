# Original pitch — "Pocket Foundry" (owner, 2026-10-20)

Kept as written for reference. `PLAN.md` supersedes it; `docs/concept-v1.md` is the first
expansion that turned it into Foldworks.

---

Pocket Foundry is an incremental factory game about turning a tiny mining operation into an absurdly advanced production empire. It begins with ore and a hand-operated smelter, then grows into a sprawling network that can manufacture everything from ordinary tools to inventions that probably shouldn't exist.

The aim isn't just to make the numbers go up. It's to give players an increasingly capable factory to design, automate, optimize, and rebuild.

## The main loop

1. Extract materials from a small deposit—initially by clicking, later with automated miners.
2. Process them through machines: smelt ore, press metal into plates, assemble parts.
3. Fulfill orders to earn money, research, and reputation.
4. Expand and improve the factory with new machines, belts, storage, power, and technology.
5. Solve new production problems as recipes become longer and resources scarcer.
6. Eventually, reorganize the company to earn permanent blueprints and begin a stronger run.

A basic production chain might be:
Iron ore → iron ingots → plates → gears → machines → automatic miners

Later chains could involve chemicals, electronics, synthetic materials, and deliberately silly products like an Autonomous Sandwich Engineer or a Portable Sunset Generator.

## The factory-design game

Give the player a grid where machines and logistics occupy space. A production line might need miners, smelters, presses, assemblers, conveyors, splitters, mergers, and storage buffers.

The challenge comes from trade-offs:

- A compact factory uses less space but may be harder to expand.
- Long conveyor routes are easy to understand but create bottlenecks.
- Bigger machines produce faster but use more power.
- Upgrading one production stage may overload the next one.
- A clever layout can produce more without buying more machines.

When a line jams, the game should help explain why: "Smelters are waiting for ore" or "Assemblers are blocked by full storage." Finding and fixing those bottlenecks should be satisfying, not a guessing game.

## Give machines interesting choices

Machines can have upgrades or operating modes. For example, a smelter could be:

- Efficient: lower fuel use, slower output
- Overclocked: faster output, higher power use
- Precision: better material yield, slower production

This lets players improve a factory in different ways rather than always buying the one upgrade with the biggest number.

## Orders and goals

Orders give players a reason to make particular products, instead of simply selling everything automatically.

A customer might request:

- A steady supply of metal plates
- A large batch of gears
- A complicated product made from several production chains

Orders could offer a choice of rewards: money, research, reputation, or rare components. Keep ordinary orders available indefinitely, so players don't feel punished for missing a timer. Optional limited-time challenges can exist, but the main game shouldn't demand daily logins.

Longer-term projects can ask players to build something remarkable—a gigantic drill, a floating city, or a device that turns scrap into rare materials. These act as major milestones that require planning across several production lines.

## Long-term progression

A long-lasting game needs more than an endlessly increasing price curve. It needs new kinds of decisions at different stages.

### 1. New production tiers

Start with familiar materials, then steadily introduce new manufacturing problems:

- Early game: iron, copper, fuel, basic parts
- Mid game: steel, glass, chemicals, circuits, robotics
- Late game: exotic alloys, artificial materials, space-grade components
- Endgame: strange, expensive inventions with sprawling supply chains

Each tier should change how the player thinks about the factory—not just add a more valuable ore.

### 2. Research branches

Let players specialize, while making it possible to unlock everything eventually. For example:

- Extraction: better deposits, faster miners, less waste
- Manufacturing: faster machines, alternate recipes, better yields
- Logistics: smarter belts, larger buffers, routing tools
- Power: cheaper energy, storage, overclocking
- Commerce: better contracts and rewards

The choice matters during a run, but shouldn't permanently lock players out of content.

### 3. Company reorganizations

Once the factory reaches a major milestone, the player can choose to reset it and earn Patent Points. These buy permanent improvements: an extra starting machine, better early research, or access to alternate recipes.

A reset should feel like a strategic decision, not an order from the game. Let players continue building if they want; offer a clear preview of what resetting will grant.

### 4. Different factory scenarios

After the first playthrough, offer scenarios that change the rules:

- A tiny island with almost no room
- Rich ore deposits but limited power
- A factory where conveyor space is scarce
- Unusual starting machines or recipes
- A challenge with a fixed set of available materials

These give experienced players fresh problems to solve without requiring an entirely new game.

## Endgame and repeat play

For hundreds of hours of potential play, add systems that encourage mastery and creativity:

- Infinite production goals: increasingly difficult projects, with milestones and cosmetic rewards
- Efficiency ratings: compare factories by output, power consumption, space, or waste
- Blueprints: save layouts and reuse them in later factories
- Community blueprints: share factory designs or try other players' builds
- Challenge runs: fixed maps, unusual restrictions, or optional scoreboards
- Collections and discoveries: unlock alternate recipes, machine skins, product lore, and factory decorations

These should be different ways to play, not a checklist of chores. A player who likes optimizing layouts should have something to pursue; a player who likes watching a factory grow should be able to do that too.

## Idle play without removing the game

Offline production lets the factory keep working when the player closes the page. On returning, show what happened and where production got stuck.
